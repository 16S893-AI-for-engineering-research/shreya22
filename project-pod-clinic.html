Pod clinic

Air quality impacts of changing airline network structures: where the project stands and how it is split.

The question

Hub-and-spoke and point-to-point networks can serve the same passengers with very different flights: different aircraft, different stage lengths, and emissions released over different places. Is one structure better for air quality than the other? I want to answer this with open data only (ADR 0004), and to compare the two structures on population-weighted exposure rather than on fuel burn (ADR 0003), because fuel is a good proxy for climate but a poor one for air quality.

Two parts

Part 1 · The network-level question

Status: runs on real data for the six-airport region; exposure not started

Build both network structures for the same fixed demand and compare them. Today this runs for a six-airport region (BOS, DEN, DTW, MSP, ORD, SFO) in 2019 Q1:

  • Demand: BTS DB1B ticket sample (×10), 2,395,440 passengers.
  • Aircraft: the real T-100 aircraft mix on every segment.
  • Fuel and NOx: OpenAP synthetic flights for every aircraft type.
  • Checks: passenger conservation, route invariants, deterministic outputs, 791 tests with mutation checks, CI on every push.

How to read this (and why it is not a finding).

  • The answer moves with a modelling choice. Adding DEN as a second hub, with each airport sent to its nearest hub (SFO goes to DEN), cuts hub-and-spoke fuel from 1.24× to 1.06× of point-to-point. The structure did not change; the hub rule did. The nearest-hub rule has no decision record yet.
  • Hub-and-spoke has more flights and more passenger-legs by construction (1.6–1.8× the legs, 1.75–1.9× the flights): connecting passengers fly two legs. Fuel rises far less than flights, because trunk legs use larger aircraft.
  • The rows cover different shares of the fleet. 10% of hub-and-spoke departures have no OpenAP model, against 7% for point-to-point; those flights are left out of fuel, which understates hub-and-spoke more.
  • Demand is 61% of T-100 passengers in this region, and the point-to-point network gets a nonstop only where T-100 shows one (all 30 directed pairs here have one).
  • None of this is exposure. The project compares population-weighted exposure (ADR 0003); fuel and NOx totals say nothing about where the emissions land.

Reproducible from data/part1-region (outputs and input hashes).

What I learned from checking it: modelled fuel is 35% below a BTS-based reference, and almost all of that is demand coverage (DB1B origin–destination demand covers 61% of T-100 passengers in the region). When T-100’s own passengers are used as demand, the model reproduces T-100 aircraft-miles to 0.2% and fuel to 5%. Why coverage is low is not yet tested. Details on the Prototype page.

Part 2 · An interactive trip tool

Status: working prototype, open the trip tool

Building on the verified smallest test case: pick two airports from the 50 busiest US airports (2,450 ordered pairs, 2019 Q1), and the tool shows the trip two ways.

  • Point-to-point: the nonstop, if one is actually flown (at least weekly in T-100). If not, ADR 0007 says point-to-point stays connecting, and the tool shows that instead of inventing a flight.
  • Hub-and-spoke: via the airport DB1B passengers most often connect through for that pair.
  • For every leg: the aircraft actually flown (T-100 mix), a range check, an OpenAP altitude profile, and fuel per flight, fuel per passenger and NOx per passenger.

It is precomputed from the same code as Part 1, so Part 1’s tests cover it, and it runs as a static page with no server. Choosing aircraft and hubs is a lookup in BTS data, so there is no agent in this version: results stay reproducible and testable.

What it shows today. For BOS→SFO it finds a real nonstop (91.9% of DB1B passengers fly it) and LAX as the most-used connection (15.6% of connecting passengers). The nonstop is 2,344 nm and uses mostly 757-200s (48%) and A321neo-XLRs (38%); the route via LAX is 2,557 nm (2,264 + 293) with a different aircraft mix on each leg. These are intermediate model outputs, not findings.

Known weaknesses I found while checking it:

  • For 744 of 2,450 pairs, one structure has a leg where over 20% of departures use aircraft with no OpenAP model (Boeing 717, MD-80s). Those flights are left out of fuel, not estimated, so the two columns are not comparable; the page says so. This is why the connection can look cheaper than the nonstop (330 of 1,943 pairs).
  • For 119 pairs no connection is observed in DB1B, and the fallback hub (shortest of the top 10 hubs) can be silly for short pairs, for example DFW–DAL via DEN.
  • Some aircraft in the real mix fly legs beyond OpenAP’s nominal design range (a 737-800 on LAX–HNL). That is flagged as a model warning: it is flown in practice, so the model range, not the flight, is the doubtful part.
  • Everything is one quarter, idealized flights at 80% of maximum takeoff weight, no wind.

One honest limit: a single trip is not a network. Hub economics come from many trips sharing legs, so the tool compares this trip nonstop against via a hub, using the aircraft that really fly each leg. It is the one-trip view of Part 1, not a small version of it.

Pipeline at a glance

  1. Demand (DB1B) — done
  2. Network structures (hub-and-spoke, point-to-point) — done; aircraft-range cutoff of ADR 0007 in progress
  3. Aircraft assignment (T-100) — done
  4. Trajectories, fuel and NOx (OpenAP) — done, as a prototype
  5. Where emissions happen (gridding along trajectories) — to be done
  6. Exposure (GEOS-Chem adjoint sensitivities, already run) — to be done
  7. Health impacts — to be done

Emissions and health are not covered in this clinic. NOx totals already exist; what is missing is where it is emitted and who breathes it.
