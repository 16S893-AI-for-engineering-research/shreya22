Network structure, fuel, and who breathes the difference

Project proposal — 16.S893. Scroll top to bottom; this page is the three-minute version of the argument.

01 · The starting point

Air travel demand is not spread evenly.

Take the US as the sample system. A handful of airports carry an enormous share of passengers; most carry very little. Any network you design has to absorb this shape of demand.

02 · Two ways to serve that demand

The same demand, two different network structures.

You can funnel everything through a few consolidated hubs, or you can fly passengers directly between their origin and destination. These are not just business models — they produce different flight counts, different aircraft sizes, and different amounts of fuel burned per passenger-kilometre.

03 · The question

If we redesign the US air transport network to burn the least fuel, do we also get the best outcome for climate and for the air people actually breathe — or do those two goals pull apart?

04 · Why this isn't one knob

Fuel is a good proxy for climate. It is a poor proxy for air quality.

05 · How — four tasks

Four tasks.

01

Build the demand & network baseline

Assemble US origin–destination demand, scheduled operations, and fleet assignment into one consistent present-day picture to optimise against.

02

Re-optimise the network for fuel

Re-allocate demand across network structures — from hub-consolidated to fully point-to-point — and find the fuel-minimising configuration for each.

03

Translate emissions into impacts

Convert fuel burn into CO₂ forcing and into gridded NOₓ/nvPM emissions, then into PM₂.₅ and ozone exposure and health burden by population.

04

Map the trade-off (and its fragility)

Where do climate-optimal and health-optimal networks disagree, how large is the gap, and how much of the answer survives the gaps in publicly available data?

06 · The honest constraint

What I can see, and what I have to model around.

The pieces of this chain sit behind very different doors. Schedules and operations are open; the numbers that actually determine fuel burn are commercial or proprietary.
