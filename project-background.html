Background

Why aviation, why network structure, and why climate and air quality need to be treated as two separate outcomes rather than one.

Aviation is hard to decarbonise

Aviation is long-haul, energy-dense, and growing in demand faster than most other transport modes. Electrification is not near-term for the segments that dominate fuel burn, so in the medium term the levers are fuel (SAF), technology (airframe and engine efficiency), and operations — how the network is flown.

Network structure is an under-used lever

Most decarbonisation analysis holds the network fixed and changes the fuel or the aircraft. But the choice between hub-consolidated and point-to-point operation changes how many flights fly, how full they are, how much extra distance is routed through hubs, and how many takeoff/landing cycles occur — all of which move fuel burn.

Climate and air quality are not the same metric

CO₂ is well-mixed: a tonne is a tonne wherever it is emitted, so fuel burn is a reasonable proxy. NOₓ and non-volatile particulate matter are not. Their health consequence depends on altitude, local chemistry, meteorology, and the population living downwind. A network that burns less fuel overall can still increase exposure if it concentrates low-altitude operations over dense metros.

Where this project sits

This work treats network structure as the design variable and asks whether the fuel-optimal design is also the health-optimal design. It is a trade-off mapping exercise, not a single-objective optimisation.
